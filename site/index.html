One data structure carries the whole product

Six square faces, projected onto a sphere. Commit to this and most of the hard problems in the concept stop being hard — simulation becomes 2D array code, agent navigation becomes a navmesh on six squares instead of a sphere, and the renderer's level-of-detail tree turns out to be the same mechanism as the product's information design.

PD 1

Why this, why now

The category is dormant and the appetite is proven. SimEarth established planetary simulation as a genre in 1990 and then nobody meaningfully advanced it for thirty-five years. WorldBox — a 2D pixel map with sprite agents and almost no production value — has sold millions of copies. The demand signal is unambiguous and the supply is a decade behind.

VR has exactly one superpower flat screens cannot replicate: scale. Not immersion, not presence, not 6DoF input — scale. The felt difference between holding something and standing inside it. God games are definitionally games about being large. This is the clearest genre/platform fit in VR and nobody has cashed it in.

The art cost that historically killed this genre is now avoidable. Planetary sims need thousands of visually distinct entities, which used to require an art department. Vector-native entity art plus procedural rulesets means a small team can ship breadth.

PD 3

Three archetypes, one simulation

The dial between them is which tools we hand you, not which content we build.

ArchetypeReferenceWantsFails if
The TinkererKerbal, Factorio, Dwarf FortressSystems that bite back; legible causalityThe sim is decorative — inputs don't propagate visibly
The GardenerAnimal Crossing, ViridiA place worth returning to; slow changeSessions demand constant input; nothing persists
The VandalWorldBox, Powder ToyImmediate spectacular consequenceDestruction is abstracted into a number instead of a place

The Vandal is the acquisition channel — it clips well. The Gardener is the retention engine. The Tinkerer writes the wiki and keeps the game alive for four years. We need all three, and the same simulation serves all three if, and only if, consequences are spatial rather than statistical.

Core loop — about 90 seconds, repeatable indefinitely

Observe the planet at chest height: climate bands, ice extent, where life has taken hold. Perturb it with one deliberate act — raise a landmass, tilt the axis, drop a rock, seed a species. Descend and go look; this beat does not exist in any prior game in the genre. Read what your perturbation actually did — a coastline drowned, a herd migrating, a forest at its new treeline. Return with new intent.

The failure mode

The loop fails if the descent is a camera move. It has to be a journey, and it has to be cheap enough to do forty times an hour.

PD 5

Design pillars

The planet is an object, not a level

It has a location in your room. It occludes. You can lean around it. You can set it down. It keeps running when you aren't looking at it. Everything that makes a level feel like software — a fixed frame, a UI border, a pause state — is disallowed.

Every number is a place

No dashboards, no graphs, no stat panels. If you want to know the ocean temperature, you go put your hand in the ocean. This constraint is what forces the descent loop to carry real informational weight instead of being a scenic detour. It is also the constraint the team will push back on hardest, repeatedly, for two years. Hold it.

Legible at every distance, at a different resolution of truth

From orbit you see bands. From 5 km you see biomes and borders. From 50 m you see individuals. Each altitude tells the truth; none tells the whole truth.

PD 6

The scale transition

This is the riskiest interaction in the product, and everything else is downstream of it.

Two stable anchors: orbital, where the planet is a ~40 cm sphere at chest height and you are outside it; and surface, where the planet is the horizon and you are 1.7 m tall standing on it. The problem is the roughly millionfold scale change between them.

VariantMechanicRisk
A · Pull-throughGrab a point, pull it toward your face; the planet grows around you and swallows you. Continuous, no cut, maximally magical.Continuous self-scaling is a known vection hazard — the visual field expands while the vestibular system reports nothing.
B · Pinch-and-stepPinch a location, a diorama window opens, you step through. Discrete and well-understood.Breaks the one-continuous-world feeling; re-introduces the sense of a level being loaded.
C · TelescopePlanet never changes scale; a held lens shows the surface. Maximally comfortable.Loses the entire payoff. This is the flat-screen game with a headset strapped on.

Recommendation: prototype A and B head-to-head in week one, before any simulation work exists. Bet on A with aggressive comfort treatment — a hand-locked reference frame held rigid through the transition, ~350 ms, snap-eased, with peripheral vignetting — and ship B as both the fallback and the permanent accessibility setting.

Hard gate

If neither transition survives a 20-minute session without discomfort at n=12, this is a flat-screen game and we should know that in week two, not month eight.

PD 7

Extruded vector

Entities are flat vector shapes extruded two to four millimetres into thin slabs — pop-up-book silhouettes on real terrain. This is the right call, not an art compromise:

  • Crisp at every zoom. A vector tree is correct at 40 cm and at 40 mm. This is literally the progressive-disclosure thesis expressed as an asset format.
  • Stereo-correct. Pure camera-facing billboards are the single most common VR art failure — at arm's length, stereo vision makes flatness obvious in a way a monitor conceals. A 3 mm extrusion fixes it entirely and costs nothing.
  • Evolution becomes interpolation. A species mutating is a path morph and a palette shift, not a new asset. Speciation across a thousand-year sim is generated, not authored.
  • Breadth without headcount. Thousands of visually distinct entities from a few dozen authored path families.

The commitment has to be total — terrain, water and clouds all sitting in the same material language — or the entities read as an asset-store mistake rather than a deliberate paper diorama.

PD 8

The ruleset is the content unit

We are not shipping infinite planets. We are shipping infinite seeds × authored rulesets, and the ruleset is the thing we actually make: a set of CA equations, a biome table, a palette, an entity family, and one signature phenomenon that exists nowhere else.

RulesetChemistrySignature phenomenon
TerraWater / carbonGlacial cycles
VermisSilicate, no free waterApex megafauna that reshape terrain as they move
SeleneAirlessEverything is thermal shock and impact history
AresThin CO₂Dust storms that eat a hemisphere
The cadence

Ten thousand procedural planets under one ruleset is a screensaver. Four rulesets × infinite seeds is a game.

A ruleset is roughly a designer plus an engineer for three weeks. That is the content cadence and the live-ops story.

PD 9

Non-goals for v1

Explicitly out of scope. Each of these has killed a comparable project.

  • Multiplayer. Not in v1, and the architecture should not be contorted to preserve the option.
  • Kingdoms, war, diplomacy. WorldBox's core. We are closer to SimEarth. Adding a civ layer before the planet layer is proven is how this becomes a mediocre 4X.
  • Hand-authored planets. If a planet needs an artist, the ruleset is wrong.
  • Universal voxel destructibility. Digging is scoped to where it earns its cost.
  • Flat-screen as a co-target. A flat build is a port after ship, not a parallel design constraint — co-targeting will silently convert "every number is a place" into a dashboard.

EN 1

System thesis

One data structure carries the whole product: a quadrilateralized cube sphere.

  • Simulation becomes plain 2D array code — no spherical geodesics, no pole singularity, no icosahedral neighbour tables.
  • Neighbours are (x±1, y±1) within a face, plus one adjacency rule for the 24 face-edge seams.
  • Rendering is a per-face quadtree with chunked LOD — a well-trodden planetary renderer, not a research problem.
  • Entity positions become (face, u, v). This is the largest single cost saving in the architecture: it converts "navmesh on a sphere" into "navmesh on six squares."
  • The renderer's LOD quadtree is the product's progressive-disclosure ladder.

The alternative — a geodesic icosphere — has better area uniformity and worse everything else. Indexing, seams, texture mapping and agent navigation all get harder. Not worth it.

EN 2

Cube-sphere specification

Mapping

Uniform grid parameter s ∈ [−1,1] per axis, tangent-adjusted:

u = tan(s · π/4)                  // tan(π/4) = 1, so the range is preserved

This exists because the naive mapping over-samples face edges: for a point (1,u,0) normalized, dθ/du = 1/(1+u²), which is 1.0 at face centre and 0.5 at the edge. Uniform steps in s produce uniform steps in angle, cutting worst-case area distortion from about 1.9× to about 1.3×.

Area weights — do not skip this

The tangent adjustment is exact only along the face axes. The residual is not noise; it is a smooth systematic bias correlated with position on the face. The exact Jacobian:

dA = du dv / (1 + u² + v²)^(3/2)

Every conserved quantity — heat, water mass, biomass, insolation — must be multiplied by a per-cell area weight derived from this. Precompute once at init: four bytes per cell, nothing at runtime.

Why this one gets its own callout

Skipping it does not produce an obvious bug. It produces a planet whose climate is subtly, permanently wrong in eight symmetric patches — and the team finds out six months in, when someone asks why deserts always form in the same relative position on every seed.

Corners

The eight cube corners have three neighbours, not four. Rather than hand-authoring 24 seam orientations — a classic source of long-lived off-by-one bugs — make the neighbour lookup generic by round-tripping through 3D: take the cell's direction vector, step in a tangent direction, re-project to (face, i, j). Bake the result into a static index array at init. Pay once, never think about it again.

The prototype does exactly this, and it produced the correct topology on the first run: 764 seam vertex groups, being 12 edges × 63 interior vertices plus 8 three-way corners.

EN 3

Layer architecture

Four layers, deliberately decoupled. The failure mode we are designing against is a monolith where an agent-AI spike drops a VR frame.

LayerWhereTickContents
L4 AgentsCPU, jobified10 HzThousands of entities, positions as (face,u,v)
L3 Voxel skinGPU, sparseon demandNear-surface bricks only, where digging occurs
L2 Field simulationGPU compute8–15 HzTectonics, climate, hydrology, biome CA
L1 Cube-sphere baseStaticonceHeightfield, area weights, neighbour tables

L2 writes to textures; L4 reads them and writes back sparsely — a settlement stamps its cell, a herd depresses local biomass. No layer calls up. Every layer runs on its own fixed tick with render-side interpolation, so no simulation spike can ever cause a dropped frame: the renderer draws the last completed state, always.

Field state is the cheap part of this product. At 6 × 256² = 393,216 cells — roughly 48× the 1990 original — a full tick is a handful of compute passes. Do not over-engineer it, and do not let it drive the platform decision.

EN 6

Voxel skin — scope this hard

The Womp-style sculpting model — GPU signed distance fields with real-time remeshing via surface nets or dual contouring — is genuinely the right feel for terrain sculpting, and it is a multi-month subsystem in its own right. A dense SDF at planet scale is not affordable on any target under consideration.

Proposal: a voxel skin over a heightfield core. The planet is a displaced cube-sphere heightfield covering effectively the entire surface. Sparse voxel bricks are allocated only where someone digs — caves, tunnels, overhangs, the Vermis burrow network — keyed by quadtree node and resident only near the player.

Heightfields cannot represent overhangs. That is the only reason we need voxels. Pay exclusively for that.

Recommendation

L3 is out of scope for Slice 1 entirely. It is the correct architecture to plan for and the wrong thing to build before the scale transition is validated.

Two rules follow directly and are not negotiable. One: simulation runs on a fixed decoupled tick with interpolated rendering, so a tectonics spike can never drop a frame. Slice 1 total: about 10 weeks. No agents, no voxels, no persistence, one ruleset. Peak team is 2 engineers, 1 tech artist and 1 product designer — deliberately small, because the architecture is chosen so that it can be.

On sequencing

M0 is a hard gate because if the scale transition cannot be made comfortable, M1 through M3 are all wasted. Do not let M1 start "in parallel to de-risk schedule." That is how you spend three engineer-months building a substrate for a game that does not exist.

Two skills must be on the team by M1: planetary quadtree LOD rendering and XR frame-budget discipline. These are the two places where inexperience costs months rather than days.

Open questions

  • Persistence model. Seed-plus-delta is obviously right for terrain edits. It is not obviously right for simulation state, which diverges continuously and irreversibly from its seed. Do we snapshot field state periodically, or accept that a planet's history is only replayable from its edit log? This changes save size by orders of magnitude.
  • Determinism. GPU floating-point across vendors is not bit-identical. If we ever want shared or replayable worlds this must be settled early — probably fixed-point for the field sim — and it is far cheaper to decide now than to retrofit.
  • Passthrough lighting. If the planet persists on a real table between sessions, it has to match real-room lighting. Meaningful graphics work; needs an early scope call rather than a late one.

What is already running

A single-file WebXR prototype, raw WebGL2, no dependencies. It is not the game — it is the substrate both briefs depend on, built so the load-bearing claims can be checked rather than asserted.

One bug worth recording

Border vertices are duplicated per face, so each copy accumulated normals only from its own face's triangles. Positions matched exactly — no crack — but shading did not, painting a visible rectangle along every seam. It showed up on the high-relief ruleset and was nearly invisible on the smooth one.

This is exactly risk E1, and it found itself within an hour on a prototype. On a production codebase without a continuity test it would have surfaced in month five, in someone else's code.

Run it in the headset

Serve the folder over HTTP and open it in the Quest browser. The Quest treats localhost as a secure context, so adb reverse is enough — no HTTPS certificate needed. Press Enter VR; the planet appears at arm's length. Hold the trigger and move your hand to roll it, thumbstick to resize, A or X to cycle rulesets.

On desktop it runs as an ordinary web page: drag to spin, scroll to zoom.
